**Foreword**

NMG Lite translates a clinician's view of metabolic health into a structured framework that can inform the development of the Personicle PCU. Its central premise is simple: **clinicians do not interpret metabolic variables in isolation. We evaluate the whole person and the trajectory of that person over time.**

An individual's metabolic state reflects the interaction of unique biology and genetics, inherited family risk, age and sex, existing medical conditions and treatments, and the circumstances of everyday life. Diet, physical activity, sleep, occupation, tobacco and alcohol exposure, and physical or psychological stress may all influence metabolic health. These influences interact over time rather than operating as independent variables.

Insulin resistance and beta-cell dysfunction are important, interacting components in the development and progression of prediabetes and Type 2 diabetes, although their relative contributions and trajectories vary among individuals. Insulin resistance is also closely associated with central adiposity, hypertriglyceridemia, low HDL cholesterol, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, and polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS). PCOS is a common, heterogeneous reproductive-metabolic disorder in which genetic susceptibility, androgen biology, ovulatory dysfunction, adiposity, insulin resistance, and environmental factors may interact. It should therefore not be interpreted simply as the cause of a woman's broader metabolic abnormalities, nor simply as an endpoint of insulin resistance. Rather, it represents an important reproductive-metabolic phenotype that should be understood within the individual's broader clinical and longitudinal context.

Other processes may evolve partly independently. Contemporary atherogenic lipoprotein science distinguishes the insulin-resistance-associated lipid phenotype from atherogenic particle burden. LDL cholesterol, non-HDL cholesterol, apolipoprotein B, and lipoprotein(a) provide cardiovascular-risk information that is complementary to glycemia and insulin resistance. Similarly, blood pressure, kidney function, liver health, and cardiovascular disease may interact with metabolic health while following their own trajectories. Contemporary Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) thinking reinforces this interconnected view of metabolic, kidney, and cardiovascular health.

It is therefore useful to think of metabolic health not as a single linear pathway, but as **multiple interacting longitudinal trajectories**. The clinical meaning of a measurement lies not only in its absolute value, but also in the person in whom it occurs, the other domains surrounding it, the treatment and context in which it was measured, and its direction of change over time. The same value may have very different meanings as an untreated baseline, a response to therapy, or evidence of progression despite treatment.

NMG Lite is organized around this whole-person, longitudinal view. It identifies the core information a clinician initially needs, distinguishes metabolic risk from established glycemic states, provides domain-specific clinical-attention signals, and allows deeper layers of information to be surfaced when clinically relevant. Follow-up and reassessment add the dimension of time, while the India-specific evidence base provides population context. The current Green, Amber, and Red signals identify where clinical attention may be needed; they are not a validated composite risk score.

For the Personicle PCU, the opportunity is therefore not simply to identify abnormal values, but to preserve their relationships, temporal sequence, treatment context, and response to intervention so that the evolving metabolic phenotype of the individual can be understood. At this stage, the primary focus is diabetes and metabolic health. NMG Lite is intended to provide a clinically grounded starting point while allowing the Personicle to evolve as additional data, evidence, and clinical needs emerge.

# **2\. Clinical Metabolic and Glycemic Status**

NMG Lite should distinguish metabolic risk from glycemic diagnosis. Prediabetes is a defined glycemic state; increased metabolic risk may be present despite normal glycemia.

| Clinical state | Working definition |
| :---- | :---- |
| Lower metabolic risk | No major identified metabolic risk factor and glycemia below the ADA prediabetes range. |
| Increased metabolic risk | Normal glycemia may coexist with central adiposity/overweight, first-degree family history, hypertension, dyslipidemia, physical inactivity, PCOS, MASLD, cardiovascular disease, or other insulin-resistance-associated features. This is a metabolic-risk state, not prediabetes. Atherogenic lipoprotein burden may coexist but represents a complementary cardiovascular-risk domain rather than a glycemic diagnostic state. See Section 4 for additional characterization of PCOS and atherogenic lipoprotein burden. |
| Prediabetes | ADA 2026: HbA1c 5.7-6.4%, FPG 100-125 mg/dL, or 2-hour plasma glucose 140-199 mg/dL during a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test. |
| Type 2 diabetes | Established or newly diagnosed Type 2 diabetes according to ADA criteria, then characterized by treatment, glycemic trajectory, complications and organ involvement. |

**4\. Additional Metabolic Detail When Clinically Needed**

Not every patient requires every metabolic measurement at the initial clinical assessment. Additional parameters may be considered when the clinical phenotype, trajectory, discordance between measurements, or specific clinical question warrants deeper evaluation.

**Fructosamine / glycated albumin.** These markers may be useful when HbA1c is unreliable or when a shorter-term assessment of glycemia is clinically useful. Fructosamine reflects approximately the preceding 2-4 weeks of glycemia. Mayo Clinic Laboratories uses a reference interval of 200-285 µmol/L, while a large community-based Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) reference sample reported a reference interval of 194.8-258.0 µmol/L. Because reference intervals vary according to assay and population, the reporting laboratory's reference interval should be used. \[14,15\]

**Approximate fructosamine-to-HbA1c relationship.** A published regression relationship is:

**Estimated HbA1c (%) ≈ 0.017 × fructosamine (µmol/L) \+ 1.61**

This represents an estimated HbA1c equivalent and is not an American Diabetes Association (ADA) diagnostic conversion. It should not replace direct HbA1c measurement when HbA1c is reliable. Interpretation should consider concurrent glucose data, albumin/protein status, and the laboratory assay. \[22\]

**HOMA-IR.** Matthews et al. introduced the Homeostasis Model Assessment in 1985 as a method for estimating insulin resistance and beta-cell function from fasting glucose and insulin measurements. \[7\] A commonly used Homeostasis Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA1-IR) calculation is:

**HOMA1-IR \= fasting insulin (µU/mL) × fasting glucose (mg/dL) / 405**

or, when glucose is expressed in mmol/L:

**HOMA1-IR \= fasting insulin (µU/mL) × fasting glucose (mmol/L) / 22.5**

There is no universally accepted normal or diagnostic HOMA-IR cutoff because results vary according to population, insulin assay, and methodology. A South Indian study proposed a cutoff of 1.23 for its study population, illustrating population dependence rather than establishing a national Indian cutoff. NMG Lite should therefore retain fasting glucose, fasting insulin, assay context, and the calculated HOMA-IR value rather than hard-code a universal diagnostic threshold. \[7,16\]

**Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), when clinically relevant.** PCOS is a heterogeneous reproductive-metabolic disorder in which genetic, hormonal, metabolic, and environmental factors may interact; it should not be viewed simply as either a cause or a consequence of insulin resistance. In adults, contemporary criteria based on the Rotterdam framework require at least two of three features after exclusion of other relevant causes: **clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and polycystic ovarian morphology**. The 2023 International Evidence-based Guideline also permits anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) as an alternative to ultrasound for defining polycystic ovarian morphology in adults. PCOS is associated with increased glycemic and cardiometabolic risk and should remain visible within the longitudinal metabolic profile. \[27\]

**TG/HDL-C evidence note.** Studies in Indian and other populations have demonstrated associations between the triglyceride-to-High-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol (TG/HDL-C) ratio and insulin resistance or adverse metabolic phenotypes. However, proposed cutoffs vary by population, sex, and study methodology. Therefore, NMG Lite uses TG/HDL-C primarily as a metabolic phenotype and clinical-attention marker rather than as a stand-alone diagnostic threshold for insulin resistance.

McLaughlin et al. studied 258 overweight, nondiabetic adults and compared simple metabolic markers with insulin-mediated glucose disposal. A TG/HDL-C ratio of 3.0, when triglycerides and HDL-C were expressed in mg/dL, was the optimal cut point in that cohort for identifying the insulin-resistant subgroup, with 64% sensitivity and 68% specificity. \[8\]

NMG Lite therefore retains **\<2.0 as favorable, 2.0 to \<3.0 as increased attention, and ≥3.0 as high attention**. The \<2.0 and 2.0 to \<3.0 categories are NMG Lite operational clinical-attention bands rather than validated diagnostic thresholds. \[8,16\]

**Atherogenic lipoprotein burden: LDL-C, non-HDL-C, apoB, and Lp(a).** NMG Lite distinguishes insulin-resistance-associated dyslipidemia from atherogenic lipoprotein burden. TG/HDL-C may help characterize an insulin-resistant metabolic phenotype, whereas Low-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol (LDL-C), non-HDL-C, apolipoprotein B (apoB), and lipoprotein(a) \[Lp(a)\] provide complementary information relevant to atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk. Atherogenic lipoprotein burden may therefore be clinically important even when glycemia is stable or insulin-resistance markers are not markedly abnormal. \[5,25,26\]

**LDL-C, non-HDL-C, and apoB.** LDL-C remains the principal lipid measure used to guide lipid-lowering therapy and should be interpreted according to overall cardiovascular risk, diabetes, Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD), established Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease (ASCVD), and treatment status. ADA 2026 recommends an LDL-C goal \<70 mg/dL for people with diabetes aged 40-75 years at higher cardiovascular risk and \<55 mg/dL for those with diabetes and established ASCVD. Non-HDL-C and apoB provide complementary assessment of atherogenic lipoprotein burden, particularly when triglycerides are elevated or LDL-C and particle burden may be discordant. \[5,25,26\]

**Lipoprotein(a) \[Lp(a)\] and CKM context.** Lp(a) is a largely genetically determined atherogenic lipoprotein and an independent cardiovascular risk factor. Contemporary guidance recommends measurement at least once in adulthood; ≥125 nmol/L or ≥50 mg/dL is considered a risk-enhancing level. The contemporary Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic (CKM) framework further reinforces assessment of atherosclerotic cardiovascular risk alongside metabolic and kidney status. NMG Lite therefore treats atherogenic lipoprotein burden as complementary to, but not interchangeable with, insulin-resistance and glycemic phenotyping. \[25,26\]

**2-hour postprandial glucose and glucose-monitoring data.** A 2-hour postprandial glucose measurement and glucose-monitoring data may be surfaced when the clinical question requires greater detail. An ordinary postprandial glucose measurement should not be confused with the ADA diagnostic 2-hour plasma glucose value obtained during a standardized 75-g oral glucose tolerance test. Continuous Glucose Monitoring (CGM) or structured glucose-monitoring data may provide additional information regarding glucose patterns, excursions, variability, and response to meals, activity, medications, or other relevant events when clinically appropriate. \[1,4\]

# **5\. Follow-up and Reassessment**

Follow-up should be driven by clinical stability, treatment changes, symptoms and organ risk rather than by color alone. The intervals below combine guideline-supported laboratory reassessment with NMG Lite suggested clinical-contact intervals. Weekly or monthly visits are not universal ADA requirements and should be individualized.

| Clinical situation | Suggested clinical follow-up | Reassessment / laboratory logic |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Lower metabolic risk; normal screening | Routine preventive care according to age, risk factors, and local standards. | If screening is normal, ADA recommends repeat diabetes screening at a minimum of every 3 years, sooner if symptoms or risk status changes. |
| Increased metabolic risk without prediabetes | Approximately every 6-12 months; sooner if risk factors are worsening | Reassess weight/waist, blood pressure, lifestyle and relevant metabolic testing based on individual risk. This contact interval is an NMG Lite clinical suggestion, not a fixed ADA mandate. |
| Prediabetes | Approximately every 6-12 months; consider 3-6 months for very high-risk or rapidly changing phenotype | ADA: test for progression to diabetes at least annually and individualize frequency. More vigilant follow-up is appropriate at the higher end of the prediabetes range or with worsening risk factors. |
| Stable Type 2 diabetes at individualized goals | Approximately every 3-6 months | ADA: assess glycemic status at least twice yearly when stable and at goal. HbA1c reflects approximately 2-3 months of glycemia. |
| Type 2 diabetes not at goal or recent treatment change | Clinical review approximately every 1-3 months, with earlier contact when needed | ADA: assess glycemic status more frequently, for example every 3 months, after treatment changes, when goals are not met, or with frequent/severe hypo- or hyperglycemia or changes in health status. |
| Major medication initiation/titration; insulin adjustment; symptomatic hyperglycemia; recurrent hypoglycemia; acute illness; adherence/tolerance concern | Days to 2-4 weeks depending on urgency; weekly contact may be appropriate during active titration or safety concerns | Use targeted glucose/CGM, blood pressure, renal function/electrolytes or other safety monitoring according to the therapy and clinical problem. This is individualized clinical practice, not a universal fixed interval. |
| Dyslipidemia / lipid-lowering therapy initiated or changed | Follow clinically according to cardiovascular risk, treatment intensity, response, and tolerability | Recheck lipid profile 4–12 weeks after initiation or dose change, then generally annually once stable. Follow LDL-C and non-HDL-C response; apoB may be reassessed when used to characterize atherogenic particle burden. Lp(a) is generally measured at least once in adulthood and usually does not require routine serial monitoring because it is largely genetically determined and relatively stable. \[25\] |
| Diabetes without established CKD | Routine diabetes follow-up | ADA 2026: assess eGFR and UACR at least annually in all people with Type 2 diabetes, regardless of treatment. |
| Established CKD | Frequency based on eGFR, albuminuria and progression risk | ADA 2026/KDIGO: monitor eGFR and UACR 1-4 times per year according to CKD stage and progression risk; the highest-risk categories may require monitoring every 1-3 months. |

**6\. India-Specific Evidence Base**

The Indian Council of Medical Research-India Diabetes (ICMR-INDIAB) program provides a major India-specific epidemiologic foundation for NMG Lite. Its nationally representative data provide important context for diabetes, prediabetes, generalized and abdominal obesity, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and broader metabolic risk across Indian States and Union Territories.

ICMR-INDIAB-17 documented the substantial and heterogeneous burden of metabolic noncommunicable disease across India and used India-relevant definitions including Body Mass Index (BMI) ≥25 kg/m² for generalized obesity, waist circumference ≥90 cm in men and ≥80 cm in women for abdominal obesity, triglycerides ≥150 mg/dL for hypertriglyceridemia, High-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol (HDL-C) \<40 mg/dL in men and \<50 mg/dL in women for low HDL-C, and Low-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol (LDL-C) ≥130 mg/dL for high LDL-C in its epidemiologic assessment. \[9\]

ICMR-INDIAB-23 further demonstrated the importance of looking beyond BMI alone. Clinically relevant metabolic abnormalities may occur in Indian adults even when BMI is below 25 kg/m², supporting assessment of central adiposity and the broader metabolic phenotype rather than relying on body weight or BMI in isolation. \[10\]

Together, these findings support the NMG Lite approach of assessing multiple metabolic domains, including central adiposity, glycemia, blood pressure, metabolic and atherogenic lipid phenotypes, kidney status, lifestyle, family history, and existing disease, rather than using any single measurement as a surrogate for overall metabolic health.

ICMR-INDIAB data can inform population context, variable selection, prevalence benchmarks, subgroup analyses, and future India-specific calibration of NMG Lite. However, many ICMR-INDIAB analyses are cross-sectional. A future predictive NMG Metabolic Risk Score should therefore derive or validate numerical weights using appropriate longitudinal Indian outcome data matched to the specific endpoint and prediction horizon.

# **7\. Longitudinal Interpretation**

For every core variable, the clinician-facing PCU should preserve four elements: current value/state; comparison with the individual baseline; direction of change; and relevant treatment, behavior, illness or contextual events around that change. The same absolute value may have different meaning depending on trajectory and treatment exposure.

**8\. Future NMG Metabolic Risk Score: Weighting Guidance**

This section is intentionally separate from the clinical attention framework. Green/Amber/Red identifies what deserves attention now. A future numerical score should estimate the probability of a clearly defined future outcome over a specified time horizon.

A future NMG score should first define the endpoint, for example progression from metabolic risk to prediabetes, progression from prediabetes to Type 2 diabetes, or a specified cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic outcome. NMG Lite domains can serve as candidate predictors, but numerical weights should be learned from appropriate Indian longitudinal data rather than assigned solely by expert opinion.

Methodological precedents include the Indian Diabetes Risk Score (IDRS) and UKPDS Risk Engine. The original IDRS provides an India-specific precedent in which multivariable logistic-regression coefficients were translated into a practical weighted clinical score, while subsequent ICMR-INDIAB national validation demonstrated its performance across a large, geographically diverse Indian population. \[23,24\] The UKPDS Risk Engine provides a separate precedent for multivariable estimation of defined clinical outcomes in people with Type 2 diabetes. \[21\] These models demonstrate different approaches to combining clinical variables into practical risk estimates, but their coefficients should not be transferred directly into NMG Lite. A future NMG risk model should derive and validate its own weights using appropriate longitudinal Indian outcome data, with assessment of calibration, discrimination, clinical utility, and external validation before clinical deployment.
